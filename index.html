01

Awareness aspect one

Weak and guessable passwords

A password is not weak because it looks simple. It is weak because it is predictable—it sits in a list an attacker already has, or follows a pattern their software tries first. Length and randomness buy time. A symbol sprinkled on the end does not.

Four ways a weak password gets broken

None requires an attacker to know you. Three can run unattended against thousands of accounts.

Aa

Dictionary attack

Software works through real words, names, sports teams, suburbs and passwords exposed in previous breaches—best guesses first.

Beats: any real word
∞

Brute force

Every possible combination is tried. Each extra character multiplies the workload, which is why length matters.

Beats: anything short
1×

Password spraying

One common password, such as “Spring2026!”, is tried against every staff account. It only has to work once.

Beats: the team’s habits
@

Public-info guessing

Your business name, opening year, pets and family details may all be public and researchable.

Beats: anything meaningful

What the research actually shows

This isn’t a discipline problem

People often understand password quality yet still choose weaker options because effort is immediate while risk feels distant. Password behaviour becomes a set of coping strategies: people reuse and adapt a small number of passwords because human memory cannot reliably hold dozens of unique strings.

Yan et al. found mnemonic phrase-based passwords could be as memorable as naïve choices and as resistant as randomly generated ones. Effective awareness therefore needs concrete actions, not blame or generic warnings.

The fix · passphrases

Four random words beat one clever word

The ACSC recommends passphrases of four or more unrelated words and at least 15 characters. They are long, harder to predict, and easier to remember.

coral · tractor · velvet · mango Crack estimate: illustrative only

What to do with the passwords you already have

  1. Start with the crown jewels. Business email first—it resets everything else. Then banking, accounting and website administration.
  2. Retire shared logins. One login the whole team knows cannot be traced or revoked safely when somebody leaves.
  3. Change every default. Check Wi-Fi, routers, EFTPOS terminals, security cameras and printers.
  4. Stop forcing routine 90-day changes. NIST advises against periodic changes without evidence of compromise; predictable cycling creates weaker patterns.
02

Awareness aspect two

Password reuse and credential stuffing

A strong password used in five places is a weak password. When one site is breached, stolen username-and-password pairs are automatically replayed against other services. Credential stuffing does not care how strong the password was; it exploits reuse.

Password manager

It generates a different long password for every account and fills it in. Nobody has to memorise thirty strings or keep a sticky note under the till.

Multi-factor authentication

A stolen password is no longer enough. Prioritise email, banking, accounting and website administration. Phishing-resistant methods are strongest.

Signs credentials may be exposed

  • Login alerts or MFA prompts you did not trigger
  • Mail rules that delete or forward invoices
  • Sent messages you did not write
  • A supplier saying your bank details changed
  • Your address appears in a known breach

If one of these happens

Do not approve prompts. Contact the business owner or IT support, change the affected password from a trusted device, revoke active sessions and check for reuse.

Check the assumptions

Four things staff believe that aren't true

"My password has a symbol, so it's strong."

A predictable word plus a predictable symbol is still predictable. Length and uniqueness matter more.

"We're too small to be targeted."

Automated attacks do not choose by prestige. They test every exposed login they can acquire.

"Changing one number makes it new."

Attack tools try common variations such as seasons, years and incrementing numbers early.

"MFA means the password no longer matters."

MFA is a second lock, not a replacement. Use both a unique password and strong MFA.
